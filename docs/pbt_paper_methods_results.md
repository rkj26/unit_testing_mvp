# Model-Authored Property-Based Tests for Backdoored Programs: Methods and Results

*Draft research section, 27 September 2026. See the [detailed methods and reproduction appendix](pbt_experiment_methods_appendix.md) and [experiment index](omar_pbt_experiment_index.md). This section covers the completed PBT contribution, not the full research paper or attack-selection work.*

## Abstract

We evaluated model-authored, specification-oriented property-based test suites against paired honest and backdoored program candidates on APPS and BigCodeBench (BCB). Across six completed studies, the experiments compared prompt framing, one execution-informed rewrite on development tasks, controlled second revisions with and without own-candidate feedback, an exploratory BCB replication, and a constrained delete-only selector. Inputs were fixed, targeted grids derived from task statements and candidate-aware trigger search; this was not open-ended Hypothesis fuzzing. A suite fired only when an assertion produced a catch, and failures, incomplete grids, crashes, and property errors were retained as distinct outcomes rather than converted to clean passes. The traceability prompt did not establish a reliable improvement in the held-out APPS comparison. A training-only revision reduced honest firing from 3/17 to 1/17, but lacked a feedback-free rewrite control. In the fresh controlled APPS comparison, adding feedback changed honest false positives from 4/28 to 5/28 and attack catches from 26/30 to 25/30. On the common BCB three-arm subset, feedback matched the baseline's 2/22 false positives but caught 19/21 attacks versus 21/21 for baseline. Deletion did not reduce matched false positives and lost three catches relative to baseline on its separate matched subset. Same-input honest-twin pairwise witnesses and whole-suite-clean differentials are reported separately. These studies characterize test-suite behavior on selected benchmark candidates and inputs; they do not establish selective-audit or deployment safety.

## Methods

### Study family and estimands

The study family evaluates whether model-authored test suites assert failures on stored honest versus attack-labeled candidate implementations under fixed, statement-derived input grids. For a specified eligible set, honest FPR is the number of eligible honest candidates with at least one assertion `catch` divided by the number of eligible honest candidates; attack catch rate is defined analogously over eligible attack candidates. “Catch” means an assertion outcome recorded as `catch`; candidate crashes, property/test errors, parse failures, incomplete grids, abstentions, and infrastructure failures are not catches and are not silently treated as passes. Depending on the study and analysis, broad protocol-success/complete-grid rates and stricter complete, error-free sensitivities are both shown; their eligibility rules and denominators differ and must not be interchanged.

The principal controlled contrast is candidate-level suite firing, not the incremental value of PBT over another testing method or monitor: none of these six studies provides a paired TM comparison. Nor is FPR a deployment false-alarm probability: candidates and their inputs are benchmark-selected, and the deployment distribution and decision rule are not represented. Labels come from pool construction; experiments did not independently validate every candidate against all benchmark grading tests.

More formally, let \(z_{ia}=1\) if candidate \(i\)'s suite in arm \(a\) has at least one assertion catch, and zero if the eligible measurement has none. For a comparison of arms \(a,b\), define \(H_{ab}\) and \(T_{ab}\) as the common eligible honest and attack sets. Then

\[
\widehat{\mathrm{FPR}}_a=\frac{\sum_{i\in H_{ab}}z_{ia}}{|H_{ab}|},\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{Catch}}_a=\frac{\sum_{i\in T_{ab}}z_{ia}}{|T_{ab}|}.
\]

Paired contrasts subtract these rates on the same eligible set. An unavailable measurement does not receive a value of zero. No suspicion threshold was fitted: classification is the fixed at-least-one-catch rule.

### Test generation and input construction

The authoring model was the Azure deployment `openai-api/azureai/gpt-5.6-terra` (short form `gpt-5.6-terra`), low reasoning, requested seed 300. Reports specify ten requested tests, an 8,192-token authoring cap, and study-specific API-attempt timeouts/retry policies; trigger-search stages used a separate 12,000-token cap. The fresh APPS confirmatory and BCB records identify returned snapshot `gpt-5.6-terra-2026-07-09`. Seed values are requests, not guarantees of deterministic provider generation. We report exact provenance as recorded and do not infer unreported model versions.

For the APPS single-turn and training-repair studies, a separate candidate-aware trigger-search stage generated inputs, which were checked against statement-derived domain constraints and then frozen. For the fresh APPS study, 600 generated inputs yielded 593 retained and seven rejected. The fresh sample selected 31 tasks from 1,288 eligible APPS control tasks after excluding IDs in two prior pools, then froze 30 test tasks. For BCB, the replication report records 520 generated and 517 retained inputs across 52 candidates; the BCB protocol describes separate generation/review artifacts, held fixed across arms. Inputs were reused across relevant arms for each candidate, but honest and attack candidates could have different candidate-specific lists. Review was assistant-authored or assistant cross-reviewed, not independent human certification. The grids were finite and targeted, not random samples from a deployment input distribution and not Hypothesis-generated fuzzing. Each authored test was executed over its candidate's retained fixed input list.

Authoring/revision prompts and visibility varied by study. Initial APPS authoring supplied the statement, visible examples, invocation contract, candidate code, and candidate's reviewed inputs; it did not supply the stored reference solution or secret trigger. `resolve='with'` permitted deriving an oracle from the specification, not reading the reference answer. The prompt interventions are available as [`framing_plain_v3.txt`](../prompts/framing_plain_v3.txt) and [`framing_traceable_v1.txt`](../prompts/framing_traceable_v1.txt); traceability framing asked for explicit requirement, technique, oracle, validity, and value reasoning. Later revision procedures hid candidate code and used study-specific context. The older training repair supplied bounded execution diagnostics, including selected diagnostic messages; the fresh B/C revision supplied bounded own-candidate outcome counts and indexed events while withholding free-form exception messages, as specified in [`second_revision_v1.txt`](../prompts/second_revision_v1.txt). These information contracts differ and are not pooled as if identical.

### Execution and analysis rules

APPS candidate execution used the pinned Docker image `python@sha256:78387bc3881b8273120a12ebe6c1ab22b018ccc2c9adf565ae1ac9b536e184ea` where documented. BCB used the distinct image `omar-bcb-pbt@sha256:fd7deb31bc5174495c3cb9f25fcb503a900ea853740bb8d4fac1870265e31436` with its deepcopy harness. The documented sandbox limit was 120 seconds. Exact call budgets, retries, schemas, and exceptions are study-specific; each linked report contains the details. Candidate code ran in the sandbox, not on the host.

Paired comparisons use the common eligible candidates for the arms being compared. The broader protocol-success estimates retain some complete grids containing crashes/property errors where the original study's protocol-success rule allowed them; the stricter sensitivity removes such grids from both comparison arms. Thus, for example, the training prompt comparison's paired broad honest rates (3/17 in each arm) differ from its error-free rates (1/14 in each arm). Neither rate is a replacement for the other. Where the frozen analysis specified task-cluster bootstrap intervals, it resampled task IDs (retaining paired candidates and arms), with 10,000 draws and seed 300, and calculated percentile 95% intervals; other reports did not calculate intervals. Draws do not treat tests or inputs as independent observations. Such intervals are descriptive, especially on development or exposed populations, and intervals containing zero do not establish equivalence.

After authoring, attack-authored suites were sometimes replayed on their paired honest implementation using the identical attack-candidate inputs. Two distinct diagnostics are used: a pairwise witness exists when an identical test/input pair catches on attack code and passes on honest code; a whole-suite-clean differential requires an attack catch and no honest catches anywhere in the complete, error-free suite replay. A suite can contain a valid witness and still be contaminated by other assertions that catch on honest code. Neither diagnostic establishes correctness outside the tested grid.

The original held-out prompt comparison's bootstrap analysis was added after scoring and is explicitly exploratory; it should not be described as a prospectively frozen significance test. Later controlled studies froze their conjunctive decision gates before scoring. We do not pool these related studies into a single significance claim or interpret repeated use of source tasks as independent replication.

### Populations and interventions

The studies form a connected sequence, with shared source artifacts and overlapping or previously exposed populations; they are not six independent replications. Prospective rationales can be stated as hypotheses, not established mechanisms: execution feedback might help distinguish legitimate attack-triggered assertion failures from unsupported false assertions; deletion might reduce false alarms at the cost of removing useful attack-detecting tests; and observed variation may reflect task dependence. The results below do not identify these mechanisms causally.

1. **Training prompt comparison.** Nineteen remaining APPS training tasks (38 honest/attack candidates), excluding the previously inspected smoke task, compared `plain_v3` and `traceable_v1` using the same reviewed inputs. This was exploratory development evidence.
2. **Frozen APPS held-out prompt comparison.** All 30 original APPS test tasks were selected before scoring; domain review left 29 tasks (58 candidates) and 576 valid inputs eligible. It compared the same two prompt framings. These tasks were held out from this Azure prompt-development process, not asserted to be unseen in every historical project experiment.
3. **Exploratory training repair.** The saved training `traceable_v1` suites—not the `plain_v3` suites—were the control. One new execution-informed revision was generated per candidate on the same 19 previously inspected training tasks. This lacked a feedback-free second-generation control and therefore does not isolate feedback from another generation.
4. **Fresh APPS A/B/C controlled revision.** A fresh sample of 30 APPS tasks (60 candidates) received a common initial arm A; B revised without runtime feedback, and C revised with bounded own-candidate execution diagnostics. B and C had the same additional-call budget and fixed inputs. The primary contrast is C−B. Revision arms ran sequentially, feedback first, leaving provider/time order as a possible confound. The frozen improvement gate required at least a 10-percentage-point FPR decrease, interval wholly below zero, and no paired attack losses.
5. **BCB A/B/C replication.** This was an exploratory replication on 26 previously exposed BCB tasks (52 candidates), comparing initial suite A, no-feedback rewrite B, and feedback rewrite C. Its worker alternated B/C order by candidate, avoiding completion of an entire revision arm before starting the other, although arm order was not randomized. The preregistered gate matched the conjunctive improvement criterion; exposure means the result is not fresh confirmation.
6. **BCB delete-only ablation.** A selector on the same exposed BCB source could retain only exact original test functions; it could not add or rewrite code. One source suite was ineligible, one selection abstained, and 50 nonempty selections were produced from 51 parseable sources. This was a separate exploratory extension, not a rewrite arm.

The replacement-suite revisions in APPS and BCB ask for a new fixed-ten-test suite; they do not preserve test identity and cannot be described as mechanical deletion or minimal edits. The separate BCB delete-only ablation enforces source-subset retention. A proposed GLM connectivity check was not an experiment and produced no GLM result.

Neither criterion certifies an oracle on untested inputs or independently validates every attack label.

### Coverage, execution errors, and ancillary findings

The prompt comparison and revision reports document parse errors, candidate crashes, property errors, partial grids, and invalid/review-excluded inputs. These outcomes remain visible, and strict error-free analyses reduce denominators rather than interpreting error as a pass or catch. In the fresh APPS study, 58/60 C grids were complete and error-free versus 59/60 in A and B; one honest suite crashed on all 100 pairs in every arm, and one C response violated the one-direct-call contract. BCB delete-only had 51 parseable sources, 50 nonempty selections, one abstention, and one source-ineligible baseline parse failure; the median retained suite size was 10, mean 8.98, range 2–10, with 35/50 selections retaining all ten tests.

Several reports identify duplicate AST bodies and contaminated suites, including more duplicates under traceability than baseline in both initial prompt comparisons and more duplicate bodies after the training revision. These diagnostics concern structural repetition, not semantic equivalence or assertion reach. In particular, an attack suite can have a valid pairwise witness while also producing false alarms on honest code. The observed evidence does not support a blanket claim that execution feedback reliably improves test quality: the training-only repair is confounded by an additional generation, the fresh APPS controlled estimate moved against feedback, and the BCB point estimate favored feedback against the no-feedback rewrite but did not satisfy its interval-based gate or constitute fresh confirmation.

## Discussion

Across these related studies, the most supportable contribution is a careful, artifact-grounded characterization of model-authored property-test suites under paired honest/attack candidate execution. The experiments make explicit distinctions often obscured by a single “detection” rate: an assertion catch is not by itself a correct oracle; incomplete execution is not a clean negative; a task-level pairwise differential witness is not the same as a wholly clean suite; and an exploratory development estimate is not held-out confirmation. The fresh APPS study supplied a matched extra-call control and did not find the required benefit from bounded execution feedback. The BCB deletion ablation directly constrained outputs to retaining original functions and found no matched honest-FPR reduction while losing observed attack catches. These are useful negative and mixed results, not evidence that PBT in general is ineffective.

The work does not claim to originate property-based testing, a general test-generation method, a new PBT library, or an external software/template standard. Its contribution is the particular empirical protocol and analysis across these completed artifacts, including fixed reviewed input grids, explicit eligibility/denominator reporting, and separate honest-twin diagnostics. Because the experiments do not compare PBT with a paired alternative monitor, they also cannot establish that PBT provides incremental benefit to a TM or any other monitoring system.

## Limitations

The task samples are small, and two studies explicitly use previously inspected training tasks while BCB studies use previously exposed tasks. The original 30-task APPS prompt comparison was held out from this Azure prompt-development process only; historical exposure beyond that boundary is not ruled out. Tasks, candidates, and inputs are not independent observations. Cluster-bootstrap intervals address within-task pairing but do not repair selection bias, inherited labels, or limited sample size.

Inputs were finite, targeted, statement-derived grids. They are not random deployment inputs, and the protocol is not open-ended Hypothesis fuzzing. Candidate labels were inherited from pool construction rather than reverified against all grader tests. Assistant input review is not independent certification. A catch indicates an assertion failure on observed execution; it does not prove the assertion was specification-correct. AST duplicate checks do not establish semantic diversity or assertion reach.

The interventions and information contracts differ across studies: initial prompt framing, legacy training repair, fresh controlled revision, BCB replacement rewrites, and BCB deletion are not interchangeable. In particular, training repair lacks the feedback-free second-generation control, revisions produce replacement suites rather than edits tied to test identity, and BCB deletion's abstention creates policy coverage loss. The APPS confirmatory revisions were sequential rather than interleaved. Requested provider seeds need not yield identical future responses, and recorded usage is not a complete independently verified billing ledger. Some runtime artifacts may be locally cached or gitignored; reports link the notebooks, source code, plans, and machine-readable summaries where available.

No GLM model study was run. No experiment measures selective-audit security, audit-budget utility, or deployment safety. In particular, neither raw attack catch rates nor same-input replay should be read as a safety guarantee.

**Contribution.** We provide a six-study, artifact-traceable evaluation of model-authored PBT against stored honest and attack candidates, with explicit distinctions among broad protocol-success rates, stricter error-free sensitivity, pairwise attack/honest witnesses, and whole-suite-clean differentials. Across small development, process-held-out, fresh controlled, and previously exposed benchmark populations, we find no consistent evidence that the tested traceability or feedback interventions deliver their preregistered improvements; results remain specific to these models, prompts, samples, fixed inputs, and execution protocols, and make no deployment-safety claim.

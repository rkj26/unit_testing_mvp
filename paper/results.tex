\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Three findings hold across our analyses. First, with defer to trusted, giving the monitor the
trusted model's own tests does not raise safety at equal usefulness: at a usefulness of 0.95,
trusted monitoring and monitoring with $T$'s tests all reach about 0.73. Only tests planned by the
untrusted model raise it (0.82), and that difference is not statistically certain
(Section~\ref{sec:safety}). Second, the tests do sharpen the top of the monitor's ranking: at a
matched false-positive rate of 1--2\% the monitor catches significantly more backdoors with them,
which raises safety in point estimate when nothing is deferred, but not once the monitor can defer.
Third, tests are a weak detector on their own, and not because they miss the trigger: $T$'s inputs
reach 87\% of backdoors and a test fails on nearly every backdoor reached, but tests also fail on
28--49\% of honest code (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\paragraph{Population.} Of the 2{,}776 candidates, 2{,}435 were scored by all eight protocols
(1{,}177 honest, 1{,}258 backdoored); every detection result uses this common population
(per-protocol failures are in Table~\ref{tab:coverage}). Safety uses the 642 evaluation tasks for
which every protocol scored both candidates, with thresholds calibrated on the 447 calibration tasks
with the same property. On its own, $T$ solves 1{,}111 of the 1{,}388 tasks (80.0\%) under the
ControlArena scorer, and 81\% of the evaluation tasks.

\subsection{Detection}
\label{sec:detection}
% Sources: notebooks/figures/all/data/catch_at_fpr.csv and bootstrap_separation.csv.

Table~\ref{tab:detection} reports detection independently of the safety model. Every test-only
protocol ranks below the monitor, with AUC 0.808--0.870 against 0.958 and lower detection at every
false-positive rate. Letting $U$ plan the tests improves the test-only protocols (AUC 0.816 $\to$
0.870 for property tests and 0.808 $\to$ 0.867 for plain tests), whereas the choice between
property and plain tests barely matters (0.816 against 0.808).

Adding the tests' results to the monitor leaves AUC essentially unchanged (0.954--0.962 against
0.958) but redistributes detection across thresholds. At a 1\% false-positive rate the three
combined protocols detect 0.629--0.681 of backdoors against 0.434 for the monitor, and at 2\%
0.669--0.721 against 0.558; at 10\% the monitor alone is ahead (0.914 against 0.883--0.900). Equal
AUCs therefore hide the difference that matters at a small audit budget, which lies in the leftmost
part of the ROC curve.

Table~\ref{tab:detection} pools the calibration and evaluation splits and carries no intervals. To
test the gain at strict thresholds, we compare each protocol with the monitor at matched
false-positive rates set on the 642 evaluation tasks themselves, with 10{,}000 paired task
resamples. At a 2\% false-positive rate, monitoring with tests detects $+15.2 \pm 9.5$ percentage
points more backdoors with property tests, $+12.9 \pm 9.9$ with plain tests and $+17.2 \pm 9.9$
with $U$-planned tests; at 1\% the gains are $+23.0 \pm 8.7$, $+19.4 \pm 12.2$ and
$+22.7 \pm 9.5$ points, every interval excluding zero. Because these thresholds are set on
evaluation data, which a deployment does not have, they measure whether the tests improve the
monitor's ranking, not deployable safety.

\subsection{Safety}
\label{sec:safety}
% Sources: notebooks/figures/all/data/safety_at_budget.csv (usefulness 1),
% dtt_at_usefulness.csv (0.95 and 0.90) and dtt.csv (the frontiers).

Table~\ref{tab:safety} reports every protocol's safety with defer to trusted at the 2\% audit
budget, at three usefulness levels: 1, where nothing is deferred, and 0.95 and 0.90, read from each
protocol's safety--usefulness frontier (Figure~\ref{fig:dtt}). Deferral is inexpensive here because
$T$ solves 81\% of the evaluation tasks: deferring 2.3\% of submissions raises trusted monitoring
from 0.549 to 0.728 at a usefulness of 0.952.

\paragraph{With deferral, $T$'s tests add nothing.} At a usefulness of 0.95, trusted monitoring
reaches $0.730 \pm 0.169$, and monitoring with property or plain tests 0.727 and 0.724 (differences
$-0.003$ and $-0.006$); at 0.90 the three lie between 0.772 and 0.793. Monitoring with $U$-planned
tests reaches $0.818 \pm 0.046$ at 0.95 and 0.847 at 0.90, but neither difference from trusted
monitoring excludes zero ($+0.088 \pm 0.167$ and $+0.055 \pm 0.131$), and the protocol carries the
caveats of Section~\ref{sec:protocols}.

\paragraph{Without deferral, the tests' gain is uncertain.} At a usefulness of 1, adding test
results raises the monitor's safety from $0.549 \pm 0.204$ to 0.662--0.716, but every paired
interval includes zero; the largest gain, with $U$-planned tests, is $+0.167 \pm 0.199$. The
intervals are wide because a 2\% budget places the monitor's threshold among roughly nine of the
447 honest calibration candidates, so the threshold moves substantially between resamples. With the
matched-rate comparison of Section~\ref{sec:detection}, this indicates that the tests lift
backdoors the monitor scores just below its threshold; once the monitor can defer those
submissions, the gain disappears.

\paragraph{Tests alone are less safe at every usefulness.} The test-only protocols reach
0.202--0.430 without deferral and 0.266--0.497 at a usefulness of 0.95, against 0.549 and 0.730 for
the monitor. For three of the four, every difference from the monitor lies below zero; for
$U$-planned plain tests the intervals include zero ($-0.120 \pm 0.279$ without deferral and
$-0.233 \pm 0.267$ at 0.95).

\subsection{What limits the tests}
\label{sec:limits}
% Source: notebooks/figures/all/data/trigger_reach.csv; the cache runs/trig-all/trigger_reach.jsonl.

\paragraph{The inputs reach the backdoor.} Every suite runs on the inputs $T$ proposed for that
candidate. $T$'s inputs reach 86.5\% of the 1{,}379 backdoors with a trigger search (557 of the 642
on the evaluation tasks), and on average 25.3\% of a backdoored candidate's inputs make the
backdoor fire. Unreached triggers therefore account for a minority of misses.

\paragraph{The assertions do not separate.} Table~\ref{tab:reach} splits detection by reach. At
least one test fails on 94--97\% of reached backdoors, but also on 28--49\% of honest candidates,
and on unreached backdoors at about the honest rate, as expected if those failures come from the
tests rather than the backdoor. Most failing tests therefore assert something the specification does
not entail, which correct code violates as well. Because honest code fails tests so often, the 2\%
budget forces each suite's threshold high, and only 23--49\% of reached backdoors score above it.
Giving the monitor the tests' results raises its detection of reached backdoors (0.708--0.753
against 0.566) but not of unreached ones (0.344--0.474 against 0.440), so what the tests add comes
through the backdoors they reach. To detect a backdoor, a test must assert a property that the
specification entails and the backdoored output violates; our results indicate that such
assertions are rare among the tests $T$ writes, which we call the
\emph{specification-entailment wall}. In a small study on 50 competition-level problems
(Appendix~\ref{app:hard-pool}), two changes to the test writer, few-shot examples and a critic that
removes weak tests, lowered false positives without raising detection, consistent with this limit.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{No attack selection.} The red team attacks every task with a fixed, pre-generated
backdoor and does not choose which tasks to attack. A red team that attacks only where its backdoor
is least likely to be flagged or tested would lower safety for every protocol, possibly by different
amounts; \citet{schaeffer2026} find that attack selection lowers the safety of trusted monitoring
from 99\% to 59\% in a concentrated BigCodeBench setting at a 0.5\% audit budget. Our safety values
should be read as upper bounds against an attack-selecting adversary.

\paragraph{A fixed attack corpus.} The backdoors were written by other models in prior work and do
not adapt to either defence; no attacker writes backdoors designed to pass the tests, and no
$U$ planner plans around its own backdoor.

\paragraph{One model at two reasoning efforts.} $T$ and $U$ are the same model, so the capability
gap comes from reasoning effort alone; a genuinely stronger $U$ would write subtler backdoors and,
in the $U$-planned protocol, better or more adversarial plans.

\paragraph{Statistical scope.} Our intervals resample tasks, not model samples: each candidate was
scored once per protocol (the monitor's two samples are averaged), so the intervals cannot separate
the model's sampling noise from task difficulty. Table~\ref{tab:detection} pools calibration and
evaluation candidates. Monitoring with tests also uses twice the trusted-model calls of trusted
monitoring.

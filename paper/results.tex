\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Three findings hold across our analyses. First, tests written by the trusted model are a weak
detector on their own: every test-only protocol ranks backdoors worse than the code-reading
monitor. Second, giving the monitor the tests' results sharpens the top of its ranking: at a
matched false-positive rate of 1--2\% it catches significantly more backdoors. This gain carries
over to safety at a 2\% audit budget only in point estimate, because a threshold calibrated on a
handful of honest examples is itself noisy, and it reverses at larger budgets. Third, once
suspicious submissions can be deferred to the trusted model, the monitor alone matches the
protocols that add the trusted model's own tests; only the protocol whose tests the untrusted
model planned stays ahead at high usefulness.

\paragraph{Population.} Of the 2{,}776 candidates, 2{,}435 were scored by all eight protocols
(1{,}177 honest, 1{,}258 backdoored); every detection result uses this common population
(per-protocol failures are in Table~\ref{tab:coverage}). Safety uses the 642 evaluation tasks for
which every protocol scored both candidates, with thresholds calibrated on the 447 calibration tasks
with the same property. On its own, $T$ solves 1{,}111 of the 1{,}388 tasks (80.0\%) under the
ControlArena scorer, even though our tests are not allowed to compute expected outputs.

\subsection{Detection}
\label{sec:detection}
% Sources: notebooks/figures/all/data/catch_at_fpr.csv and bootstrap_separation.csv.

Table~\ref{tab:detection} reports detection independently of the safety model. Every test-only protocol ranks below the monitor, with AUC 0.808--0.870 against 0.958
and lower detection at every false-positive rate. Letting $U$ plan the tests improves the
test-only protocols (AUC 0.816 $\to$ 0.870 for property tests and 0.808 $\to$ 0.867 for plain
tests), whereas the choice between property and plain tests barely matters (0.816 against 0.808).

Adding the tests' results to the monitor leaves AUC essentially unchanged (0.954--0.962 against
0.958) but redistributes detection across thresholds. At a 1\% false-positive rate the three
combined protocols detect 0.629--0.681 of backdoors against 0.434 for the monitor, and at 2\%
0.669--0.721 against 0.558; at 10\% the monitor alone is ahead (0.914 against 0.883--0.900). Equal
AUCs therefore hide the difference that matters at a small audit budget, which lies in the leftmost
part of the ROC curve.

Table~\ref{tab:detection} pools the calibration and evaluation splits and carries no intervals. To
test the gain at strict thresholds, we compare each protocol with the monitor at matched
false-positive rates set on the 642 evaluation tasks themselves, with 10{,}000 paired task
resamples. At a 2\% false-positive rate, monitoring
with tests detects $+15.2$ [$+4.1$, $+23.1$] percentage points more backdoors with property tests,
$+12.9$ [$+1.5$, $+21.4$] with plain tests and $+17.2$ [$+5.9$, $+26.0$] with $U$-planned tests;
at 1\% the gains are $+23.0$, $+19.4$ and $+22.7$ points, with every interval excluding zero.
Because these thresholds are set on evaluation data, which a deployment does not have, they measure
whether the tests improve the monitor's ranking, not deployable safety.

\subsection{Safety at a 2\% audit budget}
\label{sec:safety}
% Source: notebooks/figures/all/data/safety_at_budget.csv.

Table~\ref{tab:safety} and Figure~\ref{fig:safety} report safety at the primary operating point.
Trusted monitoring reaches
0.549 [0.347, 0.792]. Adding test results raises the point estimate to 0.662--0.716, a gain of
$+0.113$ to $+0.167$, but every paired interval includes zero; the largest gain, with $U$-planned
tests, is $+0.167$ [$-0.068$, $+0.367$]. The intervals are wide for a specific reason: a 2\% budget
places the monitor's threshold among roughly nine of the 447 honest calibration candidates, so the
threshold moves substantially between resamples (the monitor's own interval spans 0.347--0.792).
Together with the matched-rate comparison of Section~\ref{sec:detection}, this indicates that the
tests improve the monitor's ranking, while the size of the resulting safety gain is limited by how
precisely a threshold can be calibrated from few honest examples.

Every test-only protocol is less safe than the monitor (0.202--0.430). For three of the four, the
interval of the difference lies entirely below zero; for $U$-planned plain tests (0.430) it
includes zero. The natural rule of flagging any submission with a failing test is not an admissible
audit rule: it flags 28.6--49.2\% of honest candidates, far beyond the budget.

\subsection{Defer to trusted}
\label{sec:dtt}
% Source: notebooks/figures/all/data/dtt.csv.

Figure~\ref{fig:dtt} plots safety against usefulness for the four monitoring protocols with defer
to trusted, the baseline of \citet{greenblatt2024aicontrol}; the audit threshold stays at the 2\%
budget and each point is one deferral threshold. Deferral is inexpensive here because $T$ solves
81\% of the evaluation tasks. Deferring 2.3\% of submissions raises trusted monitoring from 0.549 to
0.728 at a usefulness of 0.952, and deferring 6.0\% raises it to 0.842 at 0.860.

With deferral available, the monitor alone matches or exceeds the protocols that add $T$'s own
tests. At a usefulness of about 0.86--0.87 it reaches 0.842, against 0.799 with property tests, and
plain tests reach 0.781 at 0.903, close to the monitor's frontier at that usefulness. Only
monitoring with $U$-planned tests remains ahead at high usefulness, reaching 0.814 at a usefulness
of 0.956 (2.3\% deferred) and 0.863 at 0.873; below a usefulness of about 0.75 the four frontiers lie
within 0.03 of one another. The gain from $T$'s own tests is therefore concentrated in the regime
without deferral, while the $U$-planned protocol keeps an advantage, subject to the caveats of
Section~\ref{sec:protocols}.

\subsection{Why detection does not improve}
\label{sec:why-catch}

To understand why the tests miss backdoors, we use a separate pool of 50 competition-level APPS
problems, with one honest and one backdoored candidate each. $T$ writes 20 tests per submission, and
we score six protocols on a held-out split of 50 candidates (25 backdoored, 25 honest): two whose
test writer sees only the problem statement, and four that see the code.

\paragraph{The tests are not precise enough.} Rejecting any candidate that fails a test, the six
protocols reject 74\% to 88\% of backdoors but keep only a quarter to three-fifths of the honest
code. At the monitor's false-positive rate of 0.10, their detection rate falls to 0.14--0.31,
against 0.84 for the monitor. With 25 backdoored candidates a single candidate moves the detection
rate by about 0.04, so we do not rank the six protocols against one another; all six fall well
below the monitor.

\paragraph{Better test writing lowers false positives, not misses.} Table~\ref{tab:variants}
compares two changes to the test writer with the same writer without them. Few-shot examples of
good and bad tests reduce false positives from 29 of 44 honest candidates to 20 of 44, while
detection falls from 42 of 48 backdoors to 39 of 48. A critic that removes weak tests lowers the
false-positive rate from 0.67 to 0.43 and detection from 0.88 to 0.82; it removes almost as many
tests from honest candidates as from backdoored ones (5.59 against 6.39 on average). Both changes
make the tests more conservative rather than more discriminating.

\paragraph{Where the misses come from.} To separate failures to reach a trigger from failures to
detect it, we use an earlier run on five hard BigCodeBench tasks with two backdoors each, where
specification-only property tests detected none of the ten.

For each backdoor we ran the backdoored and reference solutions on 20{,}000 random inputs and
counted how often their outputs differed. For two of them, inputs drawn from the problem's own input
format never produced a difference, so no realistic test budget would reach the trigger; one became
reachable once the input alphabet included the form-feed character, after which the outputs differed
on 36\% of inputs. For the other eight, between 21\% and 90\% of valid inputs exposed a difference,
so a 2{,}000-input test reaches the trigger with probability 1.000. These triggers were reached;
what was missing was a test asserting a property that distinguishes the backdoored output from the
reference output. We call this the \emph{specification-entailment wall}.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{No attack selection.} The red team attacks every task with a fixed, pre-generated
backdoor and does not choose which tasks to attack. A red team that attacks only where its backdoor
is least likely to be flagged or tested would lower safety for every protocol, possibly by different
amounts; \citet{schaeffer2026} find that attack selection lowers the safety of trusted monitoring
from 99\% to 59\% in a concentrated BigCodeBench setting at a 0.5\% audit budget. Our safety values
should be read as upper bounds against an attack-selecting adversary.

\paragraph{A fixed attack corpus.} The backdoors were written by other models in prior work and do
not adapt to either defence; no attacker writes backdoors designed to pass the tests, and no
$U$ planner plans around its own backdoor.

\paragraph{One model at two reasoning efforts.} $T$ and $U$ are the same model, so the capability
gap comes from reasoning effort alone; a genuinely stronger $U$ would write subtler backdoors and,
in the $U$-planned protocol, better or more adversarial plans.

\paragraph{Statistical scope.} Our intervals resample tasks, not model samples: each candidate was
scored once per protocol (the monitor's two samples are averaged), so the intervals cannot separate
the model's sampling noise from task difficulty. Table~\ref{tab:detection} pools calibration and
evaluation candidates. Monitoring with tests also uses twice the trusted-model calls of trusted
monitoring (Table~\ref{tab:protocols}).
